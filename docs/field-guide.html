OrgMap Lab Field Guide

One renderer, two organisations, four ways of reading them. This is the guide to what the maps say, who they are for, and what the software will not let you claim.

This is a preview, not a release. Everything here — every field, every colour, every line — is up for revision. It is being shown to a small group first, precisely so the structures can be hardened and the noise stripped out before anyone else sees them. Where a choice is still open, this guide says so rather than pretending otherwise.

01What this is

A tool for mapping the social system of an organisation, or of a community of organisations, from a data file that anyone can read.

Most org charts are drawings. Somebody arranges boxes, and the arrangement becomes the claim — who matters, who reports to whom, who is at the centre. When the organisation changes, the drawing is redrawn, and the reasoning behind the first version is lost.

OrgMap Lab is built the other way round. There is a data file that records what is true, and a schema that says what kinds of thing may be recorded and how each is drawn. The picture is generated. Change the data and the picture follows; change the schema and the same data is read differently, with nothing edited at all.

Two maps run in it today, and they are as different as two maps can be — a single farm's internal governance, and a bioregional network of independent land projects. That they run on the same renderer is the point: the pattern generalises, and a third context needs a schema file, not a fork.

02The one idea

Three moves. Everything else in the tool follows from them.

Attributes are not relationships

Being in the same valley is a fact about each organisation, not a line between them. Three organisations in Bensafrim should not produce a triangle. Belonging to a community is the same kind of fact. When these are drawn as lines they swamp the map — and they swamp it in a way that looks like a finding rather than an artefact.

A relationship is a claim, and it is typed

Every line names its own kind, as a predicate — a stable web address that explains what the line means. A line is not "a connection"; it is this organisation declares an affiliation with that one, or this role sits in that circle. The type is data, not styling, so the map can be filtered, checked, and argued with.

Views are filters over one dataset

The public map, a regional view, a governance view: all of them are the same rows read through different rules. There is never a second picture maintained by hand somewhere else.

03Reading a map

The legend, once. It is the same on both maps — only the vocabulary changes.

What a line's record tells you

More than the line can. Both ends, the predicate spelled out, who declared it, whether both sides did, whether it is confirmed, where the row came from — and two things worth naming separately:

  • Runs through. Where a relationship exists because of a particular person, the panel names them. The shared-personnel line between Mud Valley Foundation and Quinta Vale da Lama runs through Walt Ludwick, and the record says so.
  • What this publishes. The exact two-field entry the subject would carry in its own Murmurations profile — predicate and object, and nothing else. Set beside the full record, it shows the line that matters: who the relationship runs through is recorded locally and is not part of the published claim. "We share personnel with X" is the organisation's to say; naming the individual is that person's.

Also worth knowing

  • Hover a circle for everything recorded about it — its kind, how many lines are drawn to it in this view, and every attribute that has a value.
  • Hover a line for both ends, the predicate it uses (spelled out in full), who declared it, whether it is confirmed, where the row came from, and any note. Lines are thin, so each one carries a wide invisible catch area — you do not have to hit the stroke exactly.
  • Drag a circle to pull it out of a tangle. Scroll to zoom, drag the background to pan.
  • Field guide in the header, or g, opens this document in a new tab — so the map always carries its own instructions.
  • Click a circle or a line to open its full record in a panel on the right. Hovering is a glance; clicking is the record, and it stays open while you look elsewhere. Everything the selection is not about dims, so the map and the panel agree. Click the background or press esc to close.
  • The panel walks: a node's record lists every connection, and a line's record lists both ends. Click any of them to move the panel there.
  • The number under a circle counts the lines currently drawn — so it changes when you change the view. That is correct, not a glitch.
  • A circle with no lines has none of the kinds now being drawn. It has not dropped out of the community.

04If you are new at Vale da Lama

For someone joining the farm

Vale da Lama runs on Holacracy: work is organised into circles rather than departments, and people fill roles inside them. The map exists so you can see the shape of that without reading the whole governance handbook first.

Find yourself in four steps

  1. Open the lab, set Dataset to Vale da Lama (Holacracy).
  2. Leave Schema on as build-graph.mjs emits today — that is the live picture.
  3. Find your own name among the 14 people. Every line from you runs to a circle you are part of.
  4. Hover any circle to read its purpose — the one sentence that says why it exists. Those sentences are the fastest orientation to the farm there is.

What the map can tell you today

There are nine circles: VdL Farm, Farm Ops, Casa Ops, Groups & Retreats, Activities, B&B Accommodations, Restaurant, OMG (Organic Market Garden), and HFC (Holistic Farm Care). Eight lines show which circle sits inside which. The rest show which of the 14 people belong where.

Known gap

The map cannot yet tell you what anybody actually does. A person connects straight to a circle, so three roles held in one circle look identical to one — and a role nobody is filling shows as nothing at all. Switch the schema to org:Post model and the tool marks every one of those lines: 7 errors and 11 warnings, out of 26 rows. That is the argument for fixing it, made in the tool rather than in a memo.

Why the vacancies matter

The farm's Roles database holds 27 roles, and 8 of them have nobody in them — including every sub-circle Secretary. An unfilled role is exactly what a governance meeting needs to see. Today it is invisible, because the map has no place to put a role that nobody holds. That is the single change that would make this map useful in a governance meeting rather than merely informative.

05If you are new to the Barlavento community

For a member organisation

Barlavento is not one organisation. It is a set of independent land projects, farms, and initiatives in the western Algarve, each of which owns its own story. The map is an attempt to show how they connect without any one of them getting to narrate the others.

See the community in three steps

  1. Set Dataset to Barlavento community.
  2. Start on as recorded today. One organisation sits in the middle, touching all 13 others.
  3. Switch to vocabulary.md. Nothing in the data changed — but the map now draws 5 lines instead of 23, and the centre is gone.

The difference is the whole argument. Of the 23 recorded connections, 12 were one organisation recording that it convenes everybody, and 6 were simply "these two are in the same place." Eighteen of twenty-three lines were facts about single organisations, drawn as if they were relationships. Once they move onto the circles where they belong, the honest size of the recorded relationship graph is five.

The rule that matters

Nothing appears on the map that its subject did not choose to say. That is the direction of travel, and it is why the tool tracks where every row came from.

How to put yourself on the map properly

Everything currently drawn about you was recorded about you by somebody else. You can replace that with your own claim, published from your own domain:

  1. Publish a Murmurations profile on your own website, carrying the barlavento-eco tag. That is what puts you in the community's index.
  2. Your location and address are already ordinary profile fields — do not record them as connections to anybody.
  3. To declare a relationship, add an entry to your profile's relationships array with two values: a predicate saying what kind of relationship it is, and the other organisation's web address.
  4. Two predicates exist today. Use https://schema.org/affiliation for a genuine association, and the community's own …/predicates/shared-personnel/ when a person is active in both organisations.

Both sides declaring the same link makes it mutual, and the map draws it thicker. One side is enough to draw it at all — you should not be invisible because somebody else has not got round to publishing.

On naming people

The shared-personnel claim deliberately does not name the person. "We share personnel with X" is your organisation's claim to make. Naming an individual is that individual's.

06Administering the Vale da Lama data

The lab is not the source of truth here. Notion is.

The chain runs: Notion holds the circles, people, and roles → scripts/build-graph.mjs in the vdl-orgdev repository turns them into graph.json → scripts/seed.py in the lab turns that into the two CSV files the lab reads.

To change what the map says

Change it in Notion, regenerate graph.json, then re-run seed.py and press Reset to seed. Editing rows in the lab is for trying something out — those edits live in your browser only, and the next reseed discards them.

To change what the map is allowed to say

Edit schemas/vdl-current.json or schemas/vdl-target.json. Adding a node kind, a predicate, or an allowed value for a field is a few lines of JSON. Adding one to vdl-target is how you propose a change to the model without touching a single row of data.

Your standing check

After any reseed, open Validate. The baseline is 0 errors under the current schema and 7 errors plus 11 warnings under the target schema. If the first number moves, something upstream broke. If the second number falls, somebody has done the role work — which is the outcome everyone is waiting for.

Handle with care

This map names real colleagues and shows where each of them sits. It is internal governance material. There is no privacy layer in the lab yet — everyone who can open the map can see every name — so treat a shared screen or a shared link accordingly.

07Administering the Barlavento data

Two sources, and one job: make the second one disappear.

Every row carries a source:

ValueMeansWhat to do with it
self‑publishedRead from the member's own Murmurations profile Nothing. It maintains itself.
curatedRecorded about the member by somebody else, from the spreadsheet Help that member publish it themselves, then delete the row.

Today every row is curated. Nobody in the original spreadsheet declared anything about themselves. So the source column is a ledger of unfinished business, and the map's honesty improves as it empties. Hovering any line shows that row's provenance, so you can audit the ledger from the map rather than from the table.

Adding a member

You do not. They publish a profile with the barlavento-eco tag and the index finds them. If you must record something ahead of that, mark it curated, and mark it candidate if you are not certain — an unverified guess about a real organisation must never render as a fact. There is exactly one such row in the data today, and it has been waiting for confirmation since June.

Adding a new kind of relationship

A predicate is just a stable web address that explains itself. Open a pull request adding a page under docs/predicates/ in the community repository, with a fixed permalink. The page must say what the claim means, what it does not claim, and the nearest standard term. A predicate address is a permanent commitment — profiles across the wider network may point at it forever, so it can be clarified but never repurposed or deleted.

Deliberately absent

The original spreadsheet had a contact column naming an individual at each of the 14 organisations. It is not carried into this data, because nothing in the map needs it and those people did not opt in.

Two files per organisation — nodes and relationships — and a schema that governs both. This is the part most worth arguing with.

Barlavento — the four kinds

Nodes are Project, Initiative, Network, or Convener. The four recorded kinds of connection split two and two, and that split is the entire difference between the two schemas:

Vale da Lama — the reified role

Nodes are Circle and Person today; the target model adds Role. The change is small to state and large in effect: instead of a person connecting to a circle, a person fills a role, and the role sits in the circle.

RelationshipFrom → toStandard termStatus
Sub‑circlecircle → circleorg:subOrganizationOfin use

A role that exists whether or not anybody holds it is a standard idea — the W3C Organization Ontology calls it a Post, and that class exists for exactly this reason. Reifying the relationship is what lets an empty position be visible instead of rendering as nothing.

How the schema stops you

Two layers, and the first one is the one you will actually feel:

  • The editor only offers what is legal. Every governed field is a dropdown built from the schema. Under vocabulary.md the relationship editor offers two predicates; co-location is listed but cannot be chosen, because it is not a relationship. You cannot type a typo into a controlled field.
  • Validate checks the whole file. Ten rules, each naming itself: unknown predicate, wrong kind of thing at either end of a line, missing id, a line to nowhere, a line to itself, a duplicate claim, a value outside a controlled list, a deprecated predicate, and rows absorbed as attributes rather than drawn.

The rule that carries the most weight is the one about the ends of a line. "Fills the role" may only point at a role — so a person connected straight to a circle is refused rather than quietly drawn. That refusal is the whole finding about the Vale da Lama data.

09Views and controls

Four readings of two datasets, plus the dials.

The dials

  • Draw — a switch per relationship kind. Turning them off one at a time is the fastest way to see what any single kind contributes.
  • Colour by — node kind, or an attribute. Barlavento offers Region; Vale da Lama offers Status.
  • Badge by — draws a halo where an attribute is present. Barlavento offers Membership; the Vale da Lama target model offers Role type.
  • Labels — names on or off, and whether mutual pairs draw thicker.
  • Re-derive layout — throw the arrangement away and let it settle again. Worth doing after any change that alters the number of lines.

Giving the map the screen

escClose the record panel — or, with none open, restore the chrome
gOpen this field guide in a new tab
mMap only — hides the panels and the controls
pPanels on or off. The tab strip stays; clicking a tab brings them back
cControls on or off
fFullscreen

All five are buttons in the header too, the bar above the tabs drags to any height, and the framing you choose is remembered. A big change of size re-derives the layout so the graph fills the space; a small one leaves the arrangement you were reading alone.

10What is provisional — and what to tell us

This is the section this preview exists for.

Open, by design

  • Every visual treatment. How membership, region, or a confirmed pair should look is a design decision, and it has not been made. What is in front of you is a placeholder that reads clearly enough to argue about.
  • Whether four kinds are enough for Barlavento. They are the four found in the recorded data — not a considered taxonomy. If a real relationship in this community has no home among them, that is the most useful thing you could report.
  • What a browsing member actually needs. Most people will want to look something up and leave. There is no read-only view yet; everyone gets the full editor. Deciding what the simple view shows is the next real design question.

Known noise

  • Membership is on all 14 Barlavento nodes, so as a badge it distinguishes nobody. It only earns its halo once non-members appear on the map.
  • Every Vale da Lama node has status "Active", so colouring by status shows a single colour. The dial is there because the model supports it, not because it currently says anything.
  • Themes and purpose are free text. Useful on hover, uncontrolled, and not yet worth filtering on.
  • Both maps carry columns nothing renders. Website, size, and similar came along with the source data. Stripping them is easy; knowing which ones somebody relies on is not — so say if you use one.

What would help most

  1. Open the map that concerns you and try to answer a question you actually have. Tell us the question, not the bug.
  2. Name a field you would delete, and one you wish existed.
  3. If a line about your organisation is wrong, say so — that is data, and it is the fastest kind of fix.

11Running it

Two places it runs, and for a demo you want the second one.

The lab lives on a working machine, and a copy is served from the Mac mini, which is on all the time. Anyone signed in to the tailnet reaches that copy from any device, on or off the farm network:

http://mudvalleyinstitutes-mac-mini.tailb0b9f5.ts.net:8090/

It is held up by a keep-alive agent, so it survives a crash and returns at login. It does not survive an unattended reboot — the disk is encrypted and needs somebody to sign in first.

To run your own copy instead:

./run.shServes it at http://localhost:8090/
scripts/seed.pyRebuilds both datasets from their real sources
./deploy‑to‑mini.shPushes your copy to the Mini and restarts it there

Opening the file directly will not work — the page fetches its schemas and data, and browsers block that for local files. Your edits are kept in your own browser; Reset to seed discards them, and Export hands them back as files you can commit.

Adding a third map

A new context needs a schema file and two CSVs. Node kinds, attributes with their allowed values, and predicates with the kinds of thing each may connect — that is the whole contract. Nothing in the renderer knows about farms, circles, or the Algarve.
